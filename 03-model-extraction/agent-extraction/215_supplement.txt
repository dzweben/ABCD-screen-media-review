
Supplementary S1:
To account for potential selection bias and non-response, we applied sampling weights based on the American Community Survey. Weights were derived from a propensity model including age, sex, race/ethnicity, socioeconomic status, family income/structure, household size, parental employment, and region. Given that participants were recruited across 21 study sites, we accounted for site-level clustering by incorporating study sites as clusters in our survey analytical procedures.1

Supplementary S2
Measures
Anthropometric measurements
Body weight was measured using the Healthometer 844KL High-Capacity Digital Bathroom Scale (Health o Meter Professional Scales), and height with a carpenter's square steel tape measure, both taken three times by a trained research assistant. Waist circumference (WC) was measured using a tape measure over the highest point of the pelvic bone, rounded to the nearest 0.1 inch. The average WC was converted to percentiles based on reference data from the U.S. National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES III) using the R package childsds.1
Screen Time
Screen time activities included viewing/streaming TV shows or movies, watching videos (e.g., YouTube), playing video games, texting, video chatting (e.g., Skype, FaceTime), and using social media (e.g., Facebook, Instagram, Twitter). Average daily screen time was computed using the formula: [(weekday average × 5) + (weekend average × 2)] / 7. Self-reported smartphone screen use has shown a significant moderate correlation (r=0.49) with screen time measured objectively using a passive-sensing smartphone app.2
Sleep Disturbance
The Sleep Disturbance Scale for Children (SDSC) is a 26-item proxy-report questionnaire completed by parents/caregivers, using a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (never) to 5 (daily). It includes six subscales: difficulties initiating and maintaining sleep, disorders of arousal/nightmares, sleep-wake transition disorders, sleep-related breathing disorders, disorders of excessive somnolence, and hyperhidrosis during sleep.
Sleep Duration
Sleep duration categorization was informed by guidelines from the American Academy of Sleep Medicine for this age group.

Supplementary S3: 
Covariates 
We adjusted for baseline sociodemographic covariates due to their established associations with adolescent obesity. These included age and sex, which influence body composition and metabolism, as well as race/ethnicity, household income, and parental education—key determinants of health that affect access to resources and health behaviors. Physical activity was included given its known protective role against obesity. Study site was adjusted to account for regional variations in obesity-related factors. In addition, we adjusted for baseline body mass index (BMI) and waist circumference (WC) percentiles to account for initial anthropometric measures and to more accurately capture changes in BMI and WC over time. Puberty status was also included, as pubertal development is associated with increases in lean body mass and adiposity driven by gonadal sex steroid production.1 Adolescents self-reported their pubertal stage using the Pubertal Development Scale (PDS).2

Supplementary S4: 
Missing data were imputed using multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) in Stata for puberty status (n = 1,210, 16.3%), household income (n = 563, 7.6%), baseline BMI percentile (n = 32, 0.4%), parental education (n = 14, 0.2%), and physical activity (n = 5, 0.1%). Ten imputed datasets were created and pooled for analysis. 
Supplementary S5
To account for potential selection bias and non-response, we applied sampling weights based on the American Community Survey. These weights were derived from a propensity model that incorporated variables such as age, sex, race/ethnicity, household socioeconomic status, family income, family structure (e.g., married parents, single parent), household size, parents’ workforce status, and census region. Given that participants were recruited across 21 study sites, we accounted for site-level clustering by incorporating study sites as clusters in our survey analytical procedures.1, 2
