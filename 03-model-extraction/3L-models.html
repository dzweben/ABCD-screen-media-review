Screen time and mental health: a prospective analysis of the ABCD Study

Nagata JM, Al-Shoaibi AAA, Leong AW, Zamora G, Testa A, Ganson KT, Baker FC. BMC Public Health (2024) 24:2686.

DOI: 10.1186/s12889-024-20102-x

Extracted model · 18 phone/SM estimates

Mixed-effects linear regression of CBCL DSM-oriented symptom t-scores on baseline phone/SM modalities, fully adjusted

A single regression specification fit independently for every combination of 3 phone/SM IVs (texting, video chat, social media) × 6 mental-health DVs (depressive, anxiety, somatic, ADHD, ODD, conduct CBCL DSM-oriented scales) = 18 estimates of β₁. Identical sample, covariates, software, weighting, and equation across all 18; only the IV and DV change.

Design

Prospective longitudinal cohort with repeated outcome measurement. Participants were assessed at baseline (Year 0) and again at one-year (Year 1) and two-year (Year 2) follow-up visits as part of the ABCD Study's normal annual data-collection schedule.

Baseline phone/SM exposure (and the baseline CBCL t-score for whichever scale serves as the DV) is the predictor. The CBCL t-score at Year 1 and Year 2 is the outcome. Both follow-up scores enter the regression as separate rows in a long-format dataset (one row per child × follow-up wave); the model treats them as repeated measurements of the same outcome variable. The Y1 and Y2 follow-up data-collection period (2018–2020) overlapped with the COVID-19 pandemic, which is captured by the covid_period covariate.

The regression does not include a wave × IV interaction term, so β₁ is estimated as a single coefficient that is constrained to be the same at Year 1 and Year 2. β₁ therefore characterizes the texting (or video chat / social media) → CBCL relationship pooled across the two-year follow-up window, not separately at each wave.

Year 0 — baseline (2016–2018) Phone/SM hours/day measured (texting, video chat, social media — separately) · CBCL DSM-oriented baseline t-score for each scale measured (used as covariate)
Year 1 + Year 2 — follow-up (2017–2020) CBCL DSM-oriented symptom t-score (parent-reported) for each of the 6 scales: depressive, anxiety, somatic, ADHD, oppositional defiant, conduct. Each child contributes one Y1 measurement and one Y2 measurement; both enter the long-format dataset as separate rows of the outcome variable.
Sample

Source cohort: ABCD Study Release 4.0; baseline data collected 2016–2018 across 21 U.S. recruitment sites coordinated by the National Institutes of Health. The full ABCD baseline includes 11,875 children aged 9–10 years.

Analytic sample N = 9,538. Restricted to participants with complete data on baseline screen-time measures, baseline covariates, and the CBCL outcome at both Year 1 and Year 2. Excluded N = 2,337 (19.7% of baseline) for missing data on those variables (Appendix A of the paper compares included vs. excluded participants on demographics).

Demographics of the analytic sample: mean age 9.9 years (SD 0.6) at baseline; 48.8% female; 52.4% non-Hispanic White, 20.1% Latino/Hispanic, 17.3% non-Hispanic Black, 5.5% non-Hispanic Asian, 3.2% Native American, 1.5% Other.

The same N = 9,538 sample is used for every one of the 18 IV × DV estimates.

Independent variable (IV)

Baseline phone/SM hours per day FOCAL

Three separately-modeled predictors — one phone/SM modality per estimate. Each runs as the focal IV in its own model fit; the other two modalities are not included in the same regression as covariates.

Instrument: ABCD Youth Screen Time Survey — administered to youth at baseline. Asks how many hours per weekday and per weekend day they spent on each of six screen modalities (excluding school use). The three phone/SM-relevant items are texting, video chatting (e.g., Skype, FaceTime), and social media (e.g., Facebook, Instagram, Twitter).

For each modality, weighted average daily hours computed as (weekday × 5 + weekend × 2) / 7. Continuous (hours/day units), self-reported.

Baseline distribution in analytic sample (M ± SD): texting 0.2 ± 0.6 h/day · video chat 0.3 ± 0.7 h/day · social media 0.1 ± 0.1 h/day. Floor effects expected — most children at age 9–10 are below the 13+ minimum age for most SM platforms.

Dependent variable (DV)

CBCL DSM-oriented symptom t-score, repeated measures at Y1 and Y2 DV

Six separately-modeled outcomes — one CBCL DSM-oriented scale per estimate.

Instrument: Child Behavior Checklist (CBCL/4–18) — parent-report. 113 items rated 0–2. Items aggregate into six DSM-oriented scales: depressive, anxiety, somatic, ADHD, oppositional defiant, conduct. Raw scores converted to t-scores (mean 50, SD 10).

Year 1 and Year 2 follow-up t-scores enter the model. Baseline (Year 0) t-score on the same scale is included as a covariate. Each child contributes two follow-up t-score rows to the long-format dataset.

Covariates

Baseline value of the outcome scale cov

For every estimate, the baseline (Year 0) CBCL t-score on the same scale that serves as the DV is included as a covariate. Autoregressive control — lets β₁ be interpreted as the association of baseline phone/SM use with change-style follow-up symptoms net of baseline symptom level.

Age (years) cov

Continuous, measured in years at baseline.

Sex cov

Binary (female / male).

Race / ethnicity cov

5 categories: non-Hispanic White (reference), Latino/Hispanic, non-Hispanic Black, non-Hispanic Asian, Native American/Other. Entered as a set of dummy variables.

Household income cov

6 brackets: < $25K through ≥ $200K. Entered as a set of dummy variables.

Parent education cov

Binary: high school or less vs. college or more.

Data-collection period cov

Binary: pre vs. during COVID-19 (March 13, 2020 cutoff).

Study site cov (random intercept)

21 ABCD recruitment sites across 17 U.S. states. Entered as a random intercept.

Estimation choices

Statistical software

Nagata used Stata 18.0, fit via Stata's mixed procedure for mixed-effects regression.

Estimation method

Maximum likelihood — picks the parameter values that make the observed outcome data most probable under the model.

Sample weighting (ACS propensity weights)

Propensity-style weights anchored to the American Community Survey (ACS), the U.S. Census Bureau's nationwide demographic survey. With these weights, β₁ approximates the texting → CBCL relationship in a U.S.-representative sample of 9–10-year-olds.

Random intercept for study site

A single random intercept term for study site (21 sites). Soaks up site-level differences so β₁ reflects within-site relationships and the standard error correctly accounts for site clustering.

Significance threshold (α)

α = 0.05.

Multiple-comparison correction

None reported. With 18 tests at α = 0.05, roughly one false positive would be expected even if no associations existed.

Effect-modification (interaction) testing

Sex interactions all p > 0.05 (no sex-stratified estimates reported). Race/ethnicity interactions significant for some outcomes — but stratified results in Table 3 are total screen time only, not per-modality.

Sensitivity analysis

Appendix B re-fits the 18 models adding sleep duration and physical activity. Results were "similar but slightly attenuated" but per-modality numbers were not printed.

Screen media use and sleep disturbance symptom severity in children

Hisler GC, Hasler BP, Franzen PL, Clark DB, Twenge JM. Sleep Health (2020) 6:731–742.

DOI: 10.1016/j.sleh.2020.07.002

Model 1 of 2 · 54 phone/SM estimates

Linear regression of continuous sleep variables on dichotomized phone/SM use, fully adjusted (Cohen's d effect size)

A single regression specification fit independently for every combination of 6 phone/SM IVs (3 modalities × 2 day-types) × 9 sleep DVs = 54 estimates. Each fit is an OLS linear regression of the continuous sleep variable on the dichotomized phone/SM IV (≥2 hours vs. <2 hours), adjusted for the same 7 covariates, with Cohen's d computed from the adjusted group means as the reported effect size. Same sample, covariates, and equation across all 54 estimates; only the IV and DV change.

Design

Cross-sectional analysis of a single ABCD timepoint — baseline (Release 2.0). Both the IV (phone/SM use) and DVs (sleep variables) were measured at the same baseline visit. There is no longitudinal component: each participant contributes one observation, and the regression has no time/wave structure.

The analyses were pre-registered (hypotheses H1–H7 specified before data examination). Per the original pre-registration the IVs were going to be continuous, but data skew on the screen-time variables forced a switch to dichotomization at the AAP-recommended <2 vs ≥2 hours threshold.

ABCD baseline (single cross-section, ages 9–10) Phone/SM hours per day on weekdays and weekend days (IV) and 9 sleep-disturbance variables (DVs) all measured at the same visit. One observation per child.
Sample

Source cohort: ABCD Study Release 2.0; baseline (2016–2018), 21 U.S. recruitment sites. The full ABCD baseline includes 11,878 participants ages 9–10.

Analytic sample: N varies between 10,666 and 10,676 across analyses depending on which variables are missing for which children (listwise deletion). The "≥2 hours" group sizes for the phone/SM IVs are small relative to the "<2 hours" group, because phone/SM use was uncommon in the 9–10-year-old sample:

  • Weekday texting: N=10,385 (<2 hr) vs. N=291 (≥2 hr) — 2.8% high users
  • Weekend texting: N=10,264 (<2 hr) vs. N=410 (≥2 hr) — 3.8%
  • Weekday social media: N=10,516 vs. N=160 — 1.5%
  • Weekend social media: N=10,438 vs. N=236 — 2.2%
  • Weekday video chat: N=10,448 vs. N=223 — 2.1%
  • Weekend video chat: N=10,322 vs. N=344 — 3.2%

Demographics: mean age 9.91 years (SD 0.62); 52% female; 53% White, 20% Hispanic/Latinx, 18% Black, 5% Asian, 2% Indigenous American. Middle to upper class on average (mean parent education 16.61 years; mean family income $103,309; 69% with currently employed parent).

Some children in the sample were siblings or twins; sensitivity analyses with vs. without siblings/twins yielded similar findings, so the paper presents results in the full sample.

Independent variable (IV)

Phone/SM use, dichotomized at ≥2 vs. <2 hours/day FOCAL

Six separately-modeled binary predictors — one per (modality × day-type) combination. Each runs as the focal IV in its own regression fit; the other five are not in the same regression as covariates.

Instrument: 12-item ABCD screen-time questionnaire administered at baseline. Six modalities (TV, videos like YouTube, video games, texting, social media, video chat) × 2 day-types (typical weekday, typical weekend) = 12 raw items. Response options: "none" (0), "<30 minutes" (0.25), "30 minutes" (0.5), "1 hour" (1), "2 hours" (2), "3 hours" (3), "4+ hours" (4). The three phone/SM-relevant modalities (texting, social media, video chat) generate 6 phone/SM-qualifying IVs.

Continuous responses were dichotomized into 0 (<2 hours) and 1 (≥2 hours), based on the American Academy of Pediatrics recommendation to limit recreational screen use to ≤2 hours/day. Dichotomization was a deviation from the pre-registered plan to use continuous predictors; the authors switched because the continuous variables were severely right-skewed (most children reported very little phone/SM use at age 9–10).

β₁ in the regression therefore represents a group difference (≥2 hr group vs. <2 hr group), not a per-hour slope. The reported effect size (Cohen's d) is computed from the adjusted group means and pooled SD.

Dependent variables (DVs)

Nine continuous sleep variables, each modeled as its own DV in a separate regression. All come from parent-reported instruments at baseline.

Sleep duration (hours) DV

Single item from the Sleep Disturbance Scale for Children (SDSC): "How many hours of sleep does your child get on most nights?" Response options recoded into approximate hours: 9–11 hr → 10, 8–9 → 8.5, 7–8 → 7.5, 5–7 → 6, <5 → 2.5. Sample mean = 9.00 (SD 1.11) hours.

Sleep onset latency DV

Single SDSC item: "How long after going to bed does your child usually fall asleep?" 5-point scale: <15 min, 15–30, 30–45, 45–60, >60 min. Sample mean = 1.93 (SD 0.99).

Insomnia symptom severity DV

7-item SDSC subscale (e.g., "the child has difficulty getting to sleep at night"). α = 0.73. Items rated "never" (1) to "always (daily)" (5). Sum score; sample M = 11.76 (SD 3.75).

Sleep disordered breathing DV

3-item SDSC subscale (e.g., "the child has difficulty in breathing during the night"). α = 0.37. Sum score; sample M = 3.77 (SD 1.26).

Arousal disturbances DV

3-item SDSC subscale (e.g., "you have observed the child sleepwalking"). α = 0.56. Sum score; sample M = 3.44 (SD 0.92).

Sleep-wake transition disturbances DV

6-item SDSC subscale (e.g., "the child startles or jerks parts of the body while falling asleep"). α = 0.59. Sum score; sample M = 8.18 (SD 2.63).

Excessive sleepiness DV

5-item SDSC subscale (e.g., "the child experiences daytime sleepiness"). α = 0.72. Sum score; sample M = 6.95 (SD 2.44).

Sleep hyperhidrosis DV

2-item SDSC subscale (e.g., "the child sweats excessively during the night"). α = 0.81. Sum score; sample M = 2.44 (SD 1.18).

Total sleep-wake disturbance score DV

All 26 SDSC symptom items summed (excludes the sleep-duration and sleep-latency items). α = 0.65. Sample M = 36.54 (SD 8.24). A score of 39 was used as the clinical threshold for "disturbed sleep" in the logistic models below.

Covariates

Age (years) cov

Continuous, measured in years at baseline.

Gender cov

Binary: Male / transgender male = 0, Female / transgender female = 1.

Race cov

Binary: White = 0 vs. non-White = 1. (Coarser than paper 394's 5-category coding.)

Ethnicity cov

Binary: Hispanic/Latinx = 0 vs. non-Hispanic/non-Latinx = 1.

Family income cov

Continuous on a 1–10 ordinal scale (1 = less than $5,000, 10 = $200,000 and greater).

Parent education cov

Continuous on a 0–21 ordinal scale (0 = no formal education, 21 = doctoral degree).

Parent employment cov

Binary: currently working = 1, not currently working = 0.

Cohen's d is computed from the adjusted means at IV = 0 and IV = 1 and the pooled standard deviation, not directly returned by the regression. d ≥ 0.20 is conventionally "small," d ≥ 0.50 "medium," d ≥ 0.80 "large." Most d's in this paper are below 0.20.

Estimation choices

Statistical method

Ordinary linear regression for the continuous sleep DVs. The paper does not specify the software package; analyses were pre-registered on OSF.

Estimation method

Ordinary least squares (OLS). Coefficients minimize the sum of squared residuals. Each of the 54 cells in the matrix below is its own OLS fit with the same predictors.

Sample weighting

No survey weights applied. Estimates describe the ABCD analytic sample, not a U.S.-representative population — different from paper 394's ACS-weighted approach.

Familial nesting

Some children are siblings or twins. The paper did not use a random intercept for family. Instead, sensitivity analyses with vs. without siblings/twins were run; results were similar so the paper reports the full sample without family clustering. The standard errors therefore do not formally account for sibling clustering, though the paper argues this is unlikely to materially affect inference given the similarity of the two sample versions.

Significance threshold (α)

α = 0.001 — not the conventional 0.05. The paper writes: "in order to limit false-positive findings because of multiple inferential tests, only P-values less than .001 were interpreted." This is the paper's chosen approach to multiple-comparison correction (a uniform threshold across all 54+ tests rather than a formal Bonferroni or FDR procedure).

Multiple-comparison correction

Implemented as the α threshold above (p < .001 required for interpretation), not via Bonferroni or FDR per-test adjustment. This makes the reported "significant" cells more conservative than they would be at α = 0.05.

Effect-modification (interaction) testing

The paper tested whether Black children or Hispanic/Latinx children differed from White children in the screen-use → sleep relationships, by adding race × IV and ethnicity × IV interactions. All such interaction terms were non-significant (all p > .001), so no race- or ethnicity-stratified per-modality estimates were reported.

Sensitivity analysis

Two sensitivity checks were run: (1) the analyses were repeated with siblings/twins excluded — results similar; (2) for the non-TV and total screen-time composites only, the original pre-registered continuous IV was used in additional logistic regressions, yielding ORs of 1.05–1.07 per hour for insufficient sleep. Per-modality continuous estimates for phone/SM specifically were not reported.

Model 2 of 2 · 12 phone/SM estimates

Logistic regression of binary clinical-threshold sleep outcomes on dichotomized phone/SM use, fully adjusted (relative risk)

A second analytical specification with the same 6 phone/SM IVs but binary clinical-threshold sleep DVs. Two outcomes are tested: insufficient sleep (<9 hours) and disturbed sleep (total SDSC ≥ 39). 6 IVs × 2 binary DVs = 12 estimates of relative risk. Same sample, covariates, and IV coding as Model 1; only the DV form (continuous → binary) and estimator (linear → logistic) differ.

Design

Same cross-sectional ABCD baseline analysis as Model 1, with the same IV (dichotomized phone/SM use) and same 7 covariates. The difference from Model 1 is the DV: instead of analyzing the full continuous distribution of each sleep variable, this model dichotomizes two clinically meaningful sleep outcomes at established thresholds and asks "what is the risk of being in the bad-sleep group?"

Sample

Same as Model 1: ABCD Study Release 2.0 baseline, N varies between 10,666 and 10,676 across cells, ages 9–10. Same demographic profile and same per-IV "≥2 hr" group sizes (1.5%–3.8% of the sample depending on modality and day-type).

Independent variable (IV)

Phone/SM use, dichotomized at ≥2 vs. <2 hours/day FOCAL

Identical to Model 1 — six IVs (3 modalities × 2 day-types), each binary 0/1. The same instrument, dichotomization rule, and per-IV sample sizes apply.

Dependent variables (DVs)

Insufficient sleep (binary) DV

Coded 1 if the SDSC sleep-duration item indicated 7–8 hours of sleep or fewer; 0 if 8–9 hours or more. Threshold reflects the National Sleep Foundation recommendation for 9–11-year-olds (9–11 hours/night), with the "8–9" category included as sufficient for this dichotomization. Sample base rate ≈ 14–17% depending on day type.

Disturbed sleep (binary) DV

Coded 1 if the total SDSC sleep-wake disturbance score (sum of 26 items, M = 36.54) was ≥ 39, the established clinical threshold for "likely clinically significant sleep-wake disturbances." Sample base rate ≈ 25–30%.

Covariates

Identical to Model 1: age, gender, race (binary), ethnicity (binary), family income (1–10 ordinal), parent education (0–21 ordinal), parent employment (binary).

The full model

+β₁ · phone/SM_IViFOCAL — log-odds-ratio for ≥2 hr vs <2 hr; converted to relative risk for reporting
+β₂..₈ · controlsiage, gender, race, ethnicity, income, parent education, parent employment

The estimator returns a log-odds-ratio (β₁); the paper converts this to a relative risk for the binary outcome (using the standard formula appropriate for cross-sectional prevalence data). Reported as RR in Tables 4 and 5.

Estimation choices

Statistical method

Logistic regression for the binary clinical-threshold sleep DVs. The paper converts the resulting odds ratio to a relative risk for reporting in Tables 4 and 5.

Sample weighting

None applied (same as Model 1).

Familial nesting

Not formally modeled; same sibling/twin sensitivity check as Model 1.

Significance threshold (α)

α = 0.001 (same as Model 1; serves as the paper's MC correction).

Effect-modification (interaction) testing

Same as Model 1 — race × IV and ethnicity × IV interactions tested and all non-significant at p > .001.
